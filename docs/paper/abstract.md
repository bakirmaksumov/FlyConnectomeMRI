# Abstract (conference version)

Biological connectomes are increasingly used as structural priors for artificial neural networks, yet it is rarely tested whether the *specific* biological wiring matters beyond its coarse graph statistics. We asked whether a network constrained by real *Drosophila* visual-system connectivity performs differently from matched non-biological topologies on a human brain MRI task. From the MaleCNS v1.0 connectome we extracted a 128-neuron, 989-edge local retinotopic patch of annotated columnar visual neurons and used it as a fixed, sparse propagation mask between a small CNN encoder and a linear classifier. The identical architecture was trained with a degree-preserving rewired graph (primary null model) and with Watts–Strogatz-type, Erdős–Rényi and Barabási–Albert controls, for glioma-versus-meningioma classification on the Cheng et al. Figshare dataset (171 patients, 2,134 T1-weighted contrast-enhanced slices). The main evaluation was leakage-safe cross-validation using the official Figshare outer test folds (5 folds × 5 seeds per model), in which every patient is tested exactly once. Across 25 paired fold×seed runs, the patient-level ROC AUC difference between the Drosophila graph and its rewired control was −0.002 ± 0.030 (Drosophila higher in 12 of 25 runs); at fold level it was −0.002 ± 0.008. Graph-constrained models, biological or not, achieved higher ROC AUC than a simple CNN baseline. Under the evaluated setting, the Drosophila visual connectome did not provide a measurable topology-specific advantage; the observed gain is attributable to the graph-model architecture rather than to biological wiring. No statistical significance or clinical validity is claimed.
